What the numbers say (one paragraph)

No hospitality format on this land earns its capital back within ten years at occupancies the region has ever recorded. A 20-unit glamping and adventure village costs US$3.3 M, and at the market-consistent 28 % occupancy it makes US$74 k of EBITDA on US$570 k of revenue: after the 1 % asset tax and the money needed to replace kits and furnishings it consumes US$60 k a year. A six-unit micro-resort on the hill costs US$1.6 M and breaks even in cash at 32 % occupancy only if CONFOTUR removes its taxes. Adding that micro-resort to the residential plan on shared plant lowers the plan's after-tax return from 5 % to minus 3 % at base lot prices. Rental villas built to hold, and a day park without lodging, return nothing. The residential plan stays the recommendation; the only hospitality spend the evidence supports is a two-unit prototype (US$150-250 k) run for one season to measure the one number nobody has for Rancho Arriba, occupancy at a premium rate, and a rental-management service for the villas that lot buyers build.

1. What this study answers

Whether a hospitality use of the family land (glamping, a lodge with zip lines, clubhouse, restaurant and heated pool, rental villas, an events and day park, or a small high-design micro-resort) could be a sounder or higher-margin alternative to the 42-lot residential plan, and what such a business would entail. The method is bottom-up: every cost and revenue line is built from a unit price with a source, the derivations are shown before any profitability is stated, and every option runs through the same ten-year, unlevered, after-tax cash flow with one comparison metric (section 6.1).

Lessons: (1) the Dominican mountain products that sell are simple and priced US$95-375 a night gross, and the highest rates come from few, distinctive units (domes) rather than from many rooms; (2) every anchored land project in the DR pairs lots with a hotel, restaurant or club amenity, and the amenity is what CONFOTUR can classify; (3) adventure revenue in the DR sits on a tourist base (Cabarete's cruise and resort traffic, Cap Cana's resorts) that Rancho Arriba does not have; (4) weekend-valley destinations two hours from a big city do exist (Valle de Guadalupe), but they took a decade of restaurants and wineries to form; (5) US glamping economics (ADR US$251, branded operators) do not transfer to the DR's price level, but the micro-resort pattern (few units, extreme design, high rate) is the one format worth testing.

3. Demand evidence for Rancho Arriba

  • The nearest market, San José de Ocoa: 42 active short-term rental listings, 23 % average occupancy, US$137 average rate, US$9,900 a year per listing, that is a RevPAR of about US$32 [S250].
  • Jarabacoa: 744 active listings (up 29 % in a year), average occupancy 21.1 %, average rate US$228, average revenue US$10,213 a year, RevPAR US$45, peak month December; the top quarter of listings earns US$2,007 a month or more; median listing occupancy about 18 % [S252]. Bonao (Monseñor Nouel): 282 listings, 41 % occupancy, US$104 rate, US$14,400 a year, the best occupancy in the interior because it is 1 h from the capital [S251]. La Vega province: 1,452 listings, 32 % occupancy, US$137 rate, US$14,500 a year [S137]. A realistic residential Airbnb in the DR grosses about US$1,550 a month [S255].
  • Peaks: mountain destinations run above 85-90 % at Easter [S242, S243]; Jarabacoa's visitors are mostly Dominicans on weekends and holidays, the town has more than 1,000 properties renting rooms, and its cluster wants to move beyond a weekend destination [S126].
  • Domestic travel: only 35.1 % of Dominicans travelled inside the country in the last year; travellers are 25-39 years old (42 %), 44.5 % come from the metropolitan region, and higher-income households (over RD$70,000 a month) are the ones who book hotels and short stays; domestic travel spend was RD$229.8 bn in 2024 [S239]. More than six million Dominicans make domestic trips [S241].
  • Rancho Arriba: 95 km and about 1 h 20 min from Santo Domingo by the Duarte highway and Piedra Blanca, no existing lodging base beyond Ocoa's small lodges, an eco-lot project selling at US$70/m² with a pool and helipad [S036, S127].
  • Regulation: short-term rentals pay ITBIS at 18 % and income tax; a national registry of short-term rental properties proposed by MITUR (July 2026) and a condominium superintendency bill are in preparation, with inspections and a unique code per unit [S235, S245, S246]. Lodging needs an annual MITUR operating licence, with a title copy and staff health certificates in the file [S260, S261].

4. How the costs were built

Every capex line is a unit price times a quantity, with the source of the unit price. The formulas:

  • Imported kits (domes, tents): landed cost = FOB price × 1.12 freight × 1.10 duty × 1.18 ITBIS = FOB × 1.45. Duty ranges 0-40 % of CIF by tariff line and ITBIS is 18 % on CIF plus duty [S277]; 10 % duty is an estimate for a prefabricated structure. Under CONFOTUR the first outfitting is exempt from import duties and ITBIS (Law 158-01 art. 4 c) [S002, S133], so the landed factor falls to 1.12 and locally bought equipment and furniture lose the 18 % ITBIS.
  • Decks and platforms: US$25 per square foot, the middle of the US$15-35 range, that is US$269/m² [S223].
  • Local construction: RD$46,000/m² standard finish and RD$55,000/m² first-class finish, excluding land, design and permits [S146], that is US$742 and US$887/m².
  • Staff: posts × RD$28,000 gross × 1.38 employer load × 12 months / 62. The load is the 15.3-15.5 % employer social security contribution (AFP 7.10 %, SFS 7.09 %, occupational risk 1.10-1.30 % [S274]) plus INFOTEP 1 %, the Christmas salary 8.33 %, vacation 4.17 % and an accrual of about 8 % for severance and profit-sharing; hotel and restaurant minimum wages are RD$19,320 (large firms) and RD$15,738 (small) from June 2025, rising to RD$21,840 and RD$18,409 in June 2026 [S231, S232, S233]; RD$28,000 is a blended figure above the minimum for a mixed team. The 10 % service charge on guest bills is distributed to staff by law and is treated as neutral [S232].
  • Electricity: kWh × RD$13 / 62. The regulator's reference cost is RD$14.55/kWh; residential blocks run RD$6.17-13.26 [S258]; a commercial connection is assumed near the reference. Solar at US$1,600-1,800 per kW installed for hybrid systems [S278]; the yield of 1,600 kWh per kW a year is an estimate for the site, not a sourced figure.
  • Booking costs: Airbnb's host-only fee is 15-16 % [S234]; Booking.com commissions of 15-18 % are not archived; a blended 15.5 % is applied to the 60 % of lodging assumed to come through platforms; card fees 3 % on the rest; marketing 4 % of revenue.
  • Taxes: income tax 27 % on EBITDA less depreciation (declining balance 25 % on kits, equipment and furniture, 5 % on buildings and site works, soft costs over ten years); the 1 % tax on assets is the minimum income tax and is paid when it exceeds the 27 % ISR; land is not in the asset base (it is family-held; if contributed to the company it would be taxed at 1 % too). A CONFOTUR-classified lodge pays neither for 15 years [S002, S133]. Guest-paid ITBIS and service charge are excluded from all rates used.
  • Replacement capex (cash, not tax-deductible): 8 % a year of kits, equipment and furniture (a 12-year average life: covers and soft furnishings sooner, frames later) and 1.5 % of buildings and site works (regravelling, decks, paint), half in the opening year. Version 1 carried a 3 %-of-revenue reserve, which the critic showed to be a third of the economic renewal cost.
  • Soft costs: design and engineering 8 % of hard cost; environmental study, MITUR licence and municipal permits US$60 k; launch marketing US$60 k; pre-opening payroll three months; working capital US$50 k; contingency 15 % of hard cost (the lot model uses the same 15 %).

5. How the revenue was built

  • Rates, net of ITBIS 18 % and the 10 % service charge (gross ÷ 1.28): domes US$180, tents US$110, cabins US$140. The Domes Jarabacoa's US$240-375 gross with breakfast for two [S198 summary] is US$187-293 net; Tubagua's US$150 casita and US$95 palapa, taxes included [S201], are US$117 and US$74 net; Villa Pajón RD$13-17 k gross per villa [S205]; Kinkára from US$190 [S214]; the coffee region US$52-140 [S216]. Blended H1 rate US$147 net (version 1 used US$157 by mixing gross and net comparables).
  • Occupancy: 22, 28 and 35 % against Ocoa's 23 % [S250], Jarabacoa's 21 % listing average and 18 % median [S252], Bonao's 41 % [S251] and the La Vega provincial 32 % [S137]. The check is RevPAR: the H1 base case earns US$41 per available unit-night, between Ocoa's US$32 and Jarabacoa's US$45, so the base case assumes nothing the market does not already do. Occupancy ramps at 75, 90 and 100 % of the steady state in operating years 1-3, and activities, events and day visitors ramp with it.
  • Food and drink: 2.2 guests per occupied unit spending US$28 a night beyond breakfast (Tubagua's three-meal plan is US$48 [S201]; two of three meals bought on site), plus US$8 per day visitor; cost of sales 35 %.
  • Zip line: riders = guests × 40 % take-up + day visitors × 10 %; guests = unit-nights × 2.2 guests ÷ a 2.2-night stay (US stays are 2.7 nights [S211]; Dominican weekends shorter). At 28 % occupancy that is 1,118 riders at US$42, about US$47 k. Version 1 counted guest-nights as guests and assumed 3,000 riders. Prices from Monkey Jungle US$40-80 [S208] and Selvatura US$56-80 [S210]; Monkey Jungle's volume rests on cruise and resort traffic that Rancho Arriba does not have. A sensitivity row gives the village 5,000 riders anyway.
  • Events: eight a year at US$3,500 net. Venue plus catering is 40-50 % of RD$0.8-1.5 M wedding budgets and catering carries 28 % of taxes and service [S262]: RD$0.32-0.75 M gross per event, of which the venue's margin after catering cost and taxes is about US$3-4 k (version 1 used US$6 k).
  • Day passes: 3,000 a year at US$12 net (about 60 a weekend).
  • H5: US$45 of food and drink per guest-night (private dinners), four events a year, no day visitors, occupancy 25, 32 and 40 %; its base-case RevPAR of US$87 is nearly twice Jarabacoa's average, which is the scarcity bet the format makes.

7. Reading the numbers

  1. No hospitality option beats the residential plan, and none returns its capital within ten years. The village's EBITDA yield on cost is 2 % at 28 % occupancy and 5 % at 35 %; after the asset tax and replacement capex the cash is negative at base. The residential plan, thin as it is, returns 5-10 % after tax and leaves the family with no operating business to run.
  2. The reason is scale and demand, not costs. Twenty units at a US$147 net rate and 28 % occupancy produce US$300 k of room revenue and US$570 k in total; a seven-day team, a restaurant and security cost US$315 k before a single guest arrives. Jarabacoa's listings average 21 % and Ocoa's 23 % [S252, S250]; a Rancho Arriba lodge would have to outperform both from day one just to break even in cash.
  3. Costs are dominated by plant and amenities, not by the units. Site works are US$0.71 M and amenities US$0.83 M of the US$2.52 M hard cost; the 20 units are US$0.98 M. The residential plan builds the same wells, treatment, microgrid and lane, which is why the hybrid was modelled; the hybrid shows that sharing the plant is not enough, because the lodge's own operating economics do not clear its own capital.
  4. CONFOTUR is worth less than version 1 said. Because depreciation already wipes out the 27 % income tax, the exemption's value is the 1 % asset tax (US$26 k a year for the village, US$13 k for the micro-resort) plus the duty and ITBIS relief on the first outfitting (US$0.19 M and US$0.09 M of capex) [S002, S133]. It moves the village's 5×-exit IRR from minus 26 % to minus 22 %.
  5. The activity revenue is small once counted per guest. The zip line earns US$47 k a year from 1,118 riders and costs US$0.29 M plus US$12 k of liability insurance [S224]; even at Monkey Jungle-type volumes (5,000 riders) the village never recovers its capital (IRR minus 21 % without an exit). The zip line should not be built on this demand.
  6. The micro-resort is the least bad hospitality format, not a margin engine. Six distinctive units on the best view shelf at US$260-300 net earn a 23 % EBITDA margin at 32 % occupancy and 33 % at 40 %, but that is US$64-113 k on US$1.6 M of capital, and its RevPAR bet (US$87 against Jarabacoa's US$45) is unproven anywhere in the DR interior. The Domes Jarabacoa prove the price point exists, not the volume [S198 summary].
  7. Rental villas built to hold do not pay. At a US$220 net rate and 30 % occupancy ten family-owned villas earn US$31 k of NOI on US$2.24 M, and less than nothing after tax and replacement [S137, S252]. The same villa sold turnkey to a diaspora buyer is the residential plan's best product; managing buyers' villas for a fee is a business with no capex.

8. What each option would entail

  • Licences and taxes: an annual MITUR operating licence with a title copy and staff health certificates in the file [S260, S261]; environmental authorization; a company whose sole purpose is the tourism project if CONFOTUR classification is sought, with pre-feasibility, MITUR no-objection and municipal approvals [S183, S184]; ITBIS 18 % and the 10 % service charge on guest bills [S235, S232]; income tax 27 % and the 1 % asset tax unless classified; the short-term rental registry MITUR is preparing [S246].
  • Operations: a general manager and a seven-day team of 8 (micro-resort) to 24 (village); OTA distribution at 15-16 % [S234]; food supply at 700 m in a greenhouse valley (an asset); zip-line construction and inspection to the ACCT standard Monkey Jungle cites [S208] and liability insurance of US$10 k+ a year [S224]; a heated pool's energy load (about 40 kWh a day in the model) offset by solar; replacement of covers, soft furnishings and equipment on a 10-15 year cycle.
  • Infrastructure the site lacks today: wells and a treatment plant (no sewer in the municipality), reliable power (EDESUR outages, hence the solar plant), fibre, an all-weather access lane to the hill shelf (the road study found the strip at 29 % and a 347 m top route at 12 %; the lane is 6-9 months of earthworks before any hill unit can be built).
  • Time: 18-24 months from decision to opening for the village (permits 6-12 months, build 9-12 months); 15-24 months for the micro-resort, because the hill lane comes first.
  • Prototype: two units on the field edge (one dome at US$62 k, one cabin at US$48 k), a water and power connection, a small service block, access and listing costs: US$150-250 k, of which the units are recoverable into the residential plan (as model homes or a caretaker's cottage) if the test fails.

9. Recommendation

Do not replace the residential plan with a resort, and do not add a lodge to it expecting a return: every hospitality case, with and without CONFOTUR, lowers the family's ten-year cash. Three things survive the numbers:

  1. A two-unit prototype for one high season (US$150-250 k, section 8), listed on the platforms with the greenhouse farm-to-table story, budgeted as market research and marketing for the lots. It produces the only number that matters, occupancy at a premium rate at Rancho Arriba, and the units have a second life in the residential plan.
  2. The clubhouse and pool as members-plus-guests amenities inside the residential plan, funded by lot sales and HOA fees as the concept already assumes, not as a restaurant business or a public day park: H4 shows a day park earning nothing, and the restaurant's staff is the largest fixed cost in every option.
  3. A rental-management service for the villas that buyers build, at the 15-25 % fee the market pays, with no capital of the family's at risk; that is where the H2 economics work, because the buyer carries the capital.

A zip line should not be built; a micro-resort should be revisited only if the prototype shows 40 % or better annual occupancy at US$250 or more net, or if an operator or sponsor takes the capital risk.
