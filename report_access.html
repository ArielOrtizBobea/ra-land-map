Access road to Parcela 2

Can the wooded upper parcel be reached from the bottom, or does it need an entrance from above? What the climate and the terrain allow, and what the numbers say.

1. What grade is reasonable here

Rancho Arriba receives about 1,530 mm of rain a year over roughly 130 rainy days (1961-90 normals), with May, August and October the wettest months and the August-October hurricane season producing the storms that wash out mountain roads; San José de Ocoa has a history of flood and landslide emergencies. That pushes a private access road toward the conservative end of the usual rules. The MOPC geometric-design criteria (R-012/M-012) and most Latin American subdivision codes use 12 % as the ceiling for local streets, with short exceptions to 15 %.

Longitudinal gradeVerdict for this site
up to 8 %Comfortable for any vehicle and surface; gravel survives if it is drained.
8 to 12 %Normal ceiling for a serviced lotification street. Gravel starts to rut and wash; concrete or asphalt preferred.
12 to 15 %Acceptable for short stretches (up to 100-150 m) on concrete with a broomed or ribbed surface, cross-drains (badenes) and lined ditches. The usual permitted maximum for local streets.
15 to 20 %Very short pitches on concrete only; 4x4 territory in the wet. Not something a municipality approves as a public street inside a lotification.
over 20 %Tractor or track territory. The 7.5 m strip of parcel _3 averages 29 %.

The cross-slope of the ground matters as much as the grade. A road can climb at 10 % across a hillside, but on ground steeper than 30-35 % the cut and fill grow fast, retaining walls (gaviones) appear, and the slope above the cut becomes a slide risk in October storms. Rules of thumb for the design: switchback radius of 12-15 m minimum, a 5-6 m road bed, 3 % crossfall to the uphill ditch, cross-drains every 40-60 m on grades above 10 %, and concrete on anything above 10 %.

2. Bottom access or top access

Alignments were computed on the terrain model with a maximum design grade, preferring the family's own land (parcels _3 and 2) and penalising side-hill ground above 30 %. Starting points: A the foot of the 7.5 m strip (721 m), B the greenhouse field (702 m), C the nearest point of the road mapped in OpenStreetMap (746 m), D the end of the road drawn on the official IGN map on the north side of the hill (816 m). Target: the top of Parcela 2 (842 m) and its centre.

Reading the results

  • The strip is not a road. It climbs 118 m in 405 m, a 29 % average in a 7.5 m corridor with no room for switchbacks.
  • A bottom access is physically possible but expensive. It means roughly a kilometre of switchbacks carved into the wooded 20-50 % slope of Parcela 2 itself, all in concrete above 10 %, and it consumes the parcel it is meant to serve.
  • The top option is short. From the end of the road on the north side of the hill the climb is 25-30 m over a few hundred metres, but about 70 m of it crosses the neighbour to the north, so it needs a right of way (servidumbre de paso) or a small purchase. The strip itself ends at the boundary with P. No. 328 at the top, which suggests it was laid out as a future connection to that side.

3. Caveats

  • Grades come from the Copernicus 30 m surface model resampled to 10 m. Real ground is rougher; local pitches will be worse than shown.
  • The road end and the dashed line near the top on the official map need a walk to confirm what they are and how far the road really goes.
  • A hillside road cannot be designed from a 30 m model. The drone survey (10 cm terrain model) comes first.

4. Recommendation

  1. Walk the north side: confirm where the road ends, its surface, and who owns the gap to Parcela 2.
  2. Ask the agrimensor for a servidumbre de paso option from the road end to the north edge of Parcela 2.
  3. Commission the drone survey of both parcels plus a 50 m buffer before any road design.
  4. Treat Parcela 2 as view lots served from its upper edge; keep the lower two thirds wooded for slope stability and as the view backdrop.
